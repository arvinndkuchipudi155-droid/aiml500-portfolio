Purpose and audience

This infographic helps AI/ML students, software professionals, and nontechnical stakeholders understand how training decisions consume data, computing capacity, electricity, and time. It follows a model from collection to deployment, linking each stage to its dominant resource needs. Its purpose is to support infrastructure literacy and careful interpretation of published costs, rather than estimate a new project’s procurement budget.

Main points and evidence

The selected examples cover documented model generations. Stanford HAI (2024) estimates training compute costs of approximately $78 million for GPT-4 and $191 million for Gemini Ultra. These are historical modeled estimates, not audited invoices or complete lifecycle budgets. The shared zero-baseline bars compare the same source’s cost metric. Meta (2024) separately reports 30.84 million H100 GPU-hours for Llama 3.1 405B and approximately 15 trillion pretraining tokens for the model family. GPU-hours measure aggregate accelerator work and cannot be compared directly with dollar costs. The cited Claude 3 card describes data sources and cloud infrastructure but does not disclose a total training budget (Anthropic, 2024).

Classification and relationships

The graphic uses two complementary classifications. Stages are arranged in lifecycle order so readers can follow dependencies from data to deployed behavior. Resources are grouped by the type of input or constraint: data, compute, energy, and time. Stage labels show overlaps because the categories are connected rather than mutually exclusive. Post-training, for example, needs both curated examples and accelerator work. The data budget covers acquisition, storage, cleaning, and annotation; the compute budget covers accelerators, memory, and networks. Energy and elapsed time expose operational constraints that a dollar headline can hide. Deployment is included to complete the lifecycle, but ongoing inference is kept separate from training-cost estimates.

Calculations and limitations

The energy panel multiplies Meta’s reported 30.84 million GPU-hours by a 700-watt GPU power rating: 30.84 million × 0.7 kW = 21.588 million kWh, or approximately 21.6 GWh. This is an illustrative nameplate-power proxy, not a measurement of actual electricity use or a full-facility total. Actual load varies, and host servers, networks, and cooling also consume energy. Electricity expense requires a tariff, while emissions depend on electricity sources and accounting boundaries. The time example is deliberately hypothetical: one million GPU-hours divided by 1,000 continuously utilized GPUs equals 1,000 hours, or about 41.7 days. Real schedules include setup, synchronization, interruptions, and repeated experiments. Neither illustration establishes a provider’s real electricity bill or calendar schedule.

Design rationale and professional value

The dark navy background, teal process accents, and amber cost callouts establish a consistent visual hierarchy. Numbered stages and arrowheads communicate direction; labels and explicit units ensure meaning does not depend on color alone. Separate badges identify provider-reported evidence and external estimates. Large type and a vector SVG keep the graphic sharp when enlarged. The portfolio also supplies a readable text version and linked references. This artifact demonstrates research synthesis, infrastructure reasoning, transparent quantitative communication, and original visual design. It complements the earlier algorithm guide and neural-network presentation by shifting attention to resource planning and operational tradeoffs.
