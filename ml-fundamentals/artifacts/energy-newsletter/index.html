🎯 Objective
To produce a decision-ready briefing on the Crane Clean Energy Center deal — quantifying energy output (835 MW, 24/7 carbon-free), financial scope ($16B GDP, 3,400 jobs), and competitive follow-on (~2.9 GW across three rival deals) so AI and energy leaders can assess whether a nuclear PPA model applies to their own infrastructure strategy.
⚙️ Process
Primary sources (Constellation investor filings, NRC updates, Microsoft sustainability reports, EIA grid data) synthesised into four analytical layers: deal mechanics, project milestones, competitive ripple effects, and risk factors — each stress-tested against contradictory evidence including Microsoft's own 23.4% emissions rise.
🛠️ Tools
Primary source research (Constellation Energy, Microsoft, NRC, EIA), industry data synthesis, newsletter design (HTML/CSS broadsheet layout), Claude AI (editorial structuring, clarity review, and visual hierarchy decisions).
💡 Value Proposition
Compresses a multi-stakeholder energy-finance deal into a briefing a non-technical executive can act on in under ten minutes. The competitive ripple analysis (~2.9 GW in follow-on deals within 12 months) makes the strategic pattern legible — giving leaders the framing to evaluate nuclear procurement for their own data centre portfolios.
📋 Professional Reflection

Working on this briefing produced two concrete improvements to my practice. First, I learned to cut before I write — identifying the three decisions a reader needs to make, then building backwards to include only the evidence that drives those decisions. Early drafts ran 40% longer with no gain in clarity. Second, I developed a habit of asking "what does this mean for someone making a call today?" after every data point, which transformed descriptive facts (835 MW, $16B, 3,400 jobs) into strategic signals (baseload sufficiency, economic case, political durability).

The most significant ethical choice was surfacing Microsoft's 23.4% emissions rise in the same briefing as the clean-energy commitment. A simpler version of this document would have omitted that number. Keeping it in made the briefing harder to write but more honest — and more useful to a leader who needs to hold both the ambition and the accountability in mind at the same time. That is what I now understand responsible AI leadership communication to mean: not the story that is easiest to tell, but the one that gives the reader the clearest picture of reality.
